\section{Evidence Types and Metrics}
The results are separated by what they actually observe. Functional tests check code behavior; pair-proxy studies label relations between questions; controlled answer studies stipulate whether a particular recorded answer fits a later task; HTTP studies include retrieval, selection, and provider fallback. Valid reuse divides suitable answers served by labelled valid opportunities. Incorrect acceptance divides invalid requests served from cache by labelled invalid requests; ambiguous approvals have their own denominator. A VERIFY count denotes abstention from a candidate, whereas provider fallbacks include all requests without a hit. Latency percentiles are reported with their path and measurement boundary.

\section{Historical Query-Pair and Proxy Experiments}
The initial candidate-pair smoke fixture and later QQP, PAWS, and STS-B studies used question-pair or sentence-similarity labels. Their candidate was often stipulated rather than retrieved, and no actual cached answer was judged for the new request. The filling-cache replay added TTL, LRU, and tenant partitions but marked cross-pair outcomes unknown when labels did not cover the retrieved candidate. Five overlapping seed splits are descriptive, not five independent production samples. PAWS transfer of the QQP-trained scorer was weak, and the strict pair-proxy risk selector chose reject-all at tested targets. The archived tables are retained in Appendix~\ref{app:historical}; they do not establish a deployed answer-error bound.

\section{Judge Validation and Public-Query Pilots}
The initial judge experiment evaluated 15 authored binary controls plus one ambiguous case. FLAN-T5-small answered NO for every binary control and scored 7/15, with zero recall on eight valid cases. Pinned DeBERTa NLI scored 13/15, accepting six of eight valid cases and rejecting all seven invalid controls, but gave a high acceptance score to the separate ambiguous case. This was model selection evidence, not a general answer-quality certification. On a separate unlabelled queue of 200 diagnostic and 150 random pairs, the judges disagreed on 95 and 73 rows respectively. Those 168 disagreements cannot be counted as factual errors. The saved \href{https://github.com/ABHISHEK27Y/VeriGate/blob/1cdb80a/docs/AUTOMATED_JUDGE_RESULTS.md}{judge-validation record} preserves that distinction.

A public NQ-Open dev pilot used 60 stored questions, 50 distinct incoming questions and ten exact repeats, excluding recorded development overlap. It used real MiniLM vectors and nearest-neighbour selection, but no independently validated reuse labels. In version 1, exact, static and adaptive policies accepted the ten repeats; answer-aware reuse accepted only five because floating-point cosine values slightly above one were rejected. A separately versioned numerical-tolerance fix accepted all ten repeats while retaining rejection of all 50 distinct random neighbours. Of those 50, 41 were below the retrieval threshold: the workload mostly tested distant candidates, not difficult paraphrases. Version 3 warm local lookup p95 was 58.46 ms; it excluded Redis, HTTP, generation and model loading. The \href{https://github.com/ABHISHEK27Y/VeriGate/blob/1cdb80a/docs/RESULTS_REALISTIC_PILOT.md}{pilot record} retains the failed original run and the follow-up rather than replacing old evidence.

These stages explain why later evaluations moved to answer-bearing technical tasks, competing candidates and real HTTP paths. Public input provenance alone does not make the labels independent or make an offline timing equivalent to gateway latency.

\section{Real Retrieval and HTTP Path}
The 25-probe applicability challenge used five authored families: 15 valid, five invalid, and five ambiguous requests, with three seeded answers per probe. It ran through HTTP, RedisSearch top-$k=5$, MiniLM, pinned NLI, and a controlled provider. Suitable candidates appeared in every valid shortlist, yet both baseline and guarded variants served only 4/15 valid answers; 11 valid misses came after retrieval. Both had 0/5 invalid accepts and 0/5 ambiguous approvals, 21/25 misses after candidate assessment, and 21 controlled-provider calls. \section{Latency and Resource Measurements}
The earlier 25-probe run measured subsequent HTTP completion at 341.792/1458.667 ms p50/p95 with the guard off and 155.311/279.052 ms on. It was one sequential OFF-then-ON run on a Windows machine; warm state and scheduling may differ, so this is not a repeatable speedup estimate. The fresh forward-verified run measured warm HTTP p50/p95 of 340.254/519.109 ms baseline and 154.414/329.077 ms combined, also with a controlled provider. Its reverse ordering measured 410.687/597.544 ms and 180.123/307.189 ms respectively. The runs show synchronous verifier cost can dominate local mock generation, but do not measure paid-provider savings.

The saved 30-trial prewarm benchmark used Redis Stack and delayed mock generation on one CI runner. A first NLI hit took 1334.897 ms with lazy load and 59.196 ms after prewarm; warm same-query hit p50/p95 was about 12.5/19 ms in both modes. Prewarm moved model work into startup rather than making inference free. A separate one-process memory snapshot rose by 680.9 MB from import to pinned NLI prewarm; models are process-local, so worker count matters. The saved Redis shortlist microbenchmark measured 80 local rounds of candidate-hash validation only: sequential median 3.357 ms and pipelined median 0.896 ms. It is not full HTTP latency or a scaling result.

\section{When Caching Can Reduce Cost}
Cost effectiveness is the design objective, not a measured monetary result in these experiments. Let $N$ be the number of requests in a fixed billing period, $h$ the fraction served from cache, $c_p$ the average avoided provider charge per hit, $c_g$ the average incremental gateway cost per request, and $C_f$ the fixed incremental hosting cost for the period. Under the simplifying assumption of a comparable provider workload, the net saving is
\begin{equation}
S = N h c_p - N c_g - C_f.
\end{equation}
The break-even condition is $h c_p > c_g + C_f/N$. Gateway cost includes embedding, verification, Redis, and hosting rather than treating HIT as zero-cost. In practice token counts and provider charges vary by request, so a billing study must sum the avoided charges of the particular requests that hit. The equation is an analytical model, not a fitted result.

For illustration only, suppose 100 eligible requests would each cause one provider call without caching and 30 are correctly reused. The gateway would make 70 generation calls, assuming no retries or additional provider-based judging. That is 30 avoided calls, not automatically a 30\% reduction in the total bill. Organizational sharing increases the pool of potentially repeated tasks within an authorized workspace; it does not pool private answers or cross organization boundaries.

Latency has a similar condition. Let $L_d$ be mean completion latency for a comparable direct-provider workload, $L_h$ mean gateway HIT latency, and $L_m$ mean gateway MISS latency, including generation. Ignoring bypasses for this simplified model,
\begin{equation}
L_{\mathrm{gateway}} = hL_h + (1-h)L_m.
\end{equation}
The gateway improves mean completion time only when this quantity is below $L_d$. This weighted-mean calculation must not be applied to p95 values. Slow verification can erase the benefit of avoided calls, especially when valid reuse is low. A real-provider study should therefore measure charges and latency for the same request mix, model, output limits, and operating conditions.

\section{Follow-up Audit Fixes and Controlled HTTP Demonstration}
A later audit pass fixed three verified issues without changing acceptance decisions, routing, or cache identity. The exact-revision CI failure was a stale generated artifact: the committed test-count summary still read 303/7 while the suite had grown, so the diff gate that regenerates and compares it failed; regenerating it (339/13) restored the gate. The opt-in Playwright dashboard regression and the pinned-NLI contract test were never enabled in CI and so skipped while the pipeline still reported success; dedicated jobs now set the flags, install a pinned browser outside the locked runtime set, and fail if a required test silently skips.

The third and most visible fix is the answer-source label. The dashboard previously fell through to ``generated answer'' for any provider that was neither the cache nor a mock, so a response from the development controlled-answer provider -- a fixed labelled fixture -- appeared as a genuinely generated answer. The label now comes strictly from the returned provider metadata: ``controlled evaluation fixture'' for the controlled provider, an explicit mock placeholder, ``cached answer'' for a cache delivery (without asserting the original provenance), a named real provider, or ``unavailable'' when none is reported. The earlier heuristic that inferred provenance from known answer text was removed, and an opt-in browser regression asserts that a controlled fixture is never shown as generated. Figure~\ref{fig:ui_controlled_source} shows the corrected panel for one such request: alongside the returned answer it reports the observed request journey, the measured gateway stages, the provider \texttt{controlled-eval-example-eval}, the \texttt{workspace} lookup scope, the \texttt{stored} write outcome, and ``NLI model evidence: not invoked'' because the exact shortcut returns before the verifier runs. Reading the source directly from this metadata, rather than from the answer text, is what keeps a fixture from being presented as a model-generated answer.

To demonstrate the honest reuse path end to end, the controlled-answer provider was also enabled on the organization path (development only, secret-less, rejected in production). A scripted HTTP run against a disposable gateway then reproduced the shared-reuse, isolation, workspace-denial, provider-failure-without-write, and TTL-expiry behaviours of Tables~\ref{tab:representative_cases_a}--\ref{tab:representative_cases_b} with real labelled answers instead of a mock echo, exercising the exact shortcut rather than the MiniLM/NLI semantic path (which needs model downloads and runs in CI). This is a controlled demonstration, not accuracy, cost, or production-readiness evidence.

\section{Limitations and Threats to Validity}
All technical suitability labels above are authored stipulations, not independent adjudication of real employee traffic. They do not test truth of the cached answer, prevalence of changed actions, privacy detection recall, or long multi-step tasks. The earlier 4/15 and fresh 8/15 results differ in task families and policy variants; they are not a before/after improvement on one dataset. A model score or judge disagreement is not an answer-error rate. Zero observed invalid accepts among five examples cannot support a low general error bound. The task-specific verifier has an executable pipeline but no promoted validated artifact. Real-provider compatibility, billed cost, broad concurrency, and scale remain unverified at this checkpoint. Revocation during generation prevents storage at the recorded revision but does not necessarily suppress the completed response; the semantic identity read and recency update are also separate operations.
